\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Detection of SMS Spam and Smishing}\label{sec:rel-detect}

The UCI SMS Spam Collection \cite{almeida2011} has long been the standard benchmark for SMS spam filtering, and a recent systematic review organises smishing research into user susceptibility, attack characterisation, defences and datasets \cite{pritom2026}. Systems built specifically for smishing often combine content features with checks of the links and domains in a message. DSmishSMS \cite{mishra2023dsmishsms} and Smishing Detector \cite{mishra2022detector} use hand-crafted features together with external domain and URL checks, and Sonowal \cite{sonowal2020} ranks content features with several correlation measures before classification. Such pipelines depend on external services and on features that are not fully specified, which makes exact re-implementation difficult. Neural approaches include convolutional and recurrent networks \cite{roy2020} and transformer-based message embeddings combined with ensemble classifiers \cite{ghourabi2023}. Hosseinpour and Das \cite{hosseinpour2025} combine country-specific semantic tags, structural patterns, character-level stylistic cues and phrase embeddings in one multi-channel model. On the Mendeley dataset, Mahmud et al. \cite{mahmud2024} combine a convolutional network with a bidirectional gated recurrent unit over word embeddings to separate legitimate from malicious messages, and Shim et al. \cite{shim2024} fine-tune BERT, DistilBERT and RoBERTa to separate smishing from spam and study data augmentation with persuasion-based prompts. We re-implement these two methods in their own task settings (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).

\subsection{Transformers and Large Language Models}\label{sec:rel-llm}

Pretrained transformer encoders \cite{devlin2019,sanh2019,liu2019} are the current standard for fine-tuned text classification, and instruction-tuned large language models \cite{qwen2024} can classify messages from a few examples in the prompt. Salman et al. \cite{salman2025spallm} compare open-source and commercial language models for SMS spam detection with zero-shot, few-shot, chain-of-thought and fine-tuned use; they find zero-shot prompting unreliable and fine-tuning the most effective strategy, also against perceptible and imperceptible manipulations of the text. Topcuoglu et al. \cite{topcuoglu2026} evaluate nine language models on a benchmark of real-world scams in several formats and report that the models generalise to unseen scams better than a fine-tuned BERT-based classifier, with larger models ahead of smaller ones. Two recent designs address the cost of such models. The hierarchical system of Chang et al. \cite{chang2025scamshield} lets a majority vote of lightweight classifiers decide most messages and escalates ambiguous ones to an adversarially fine-tuned LLaMA model, which shortens inference time. ElZemity et al. \cite{elzemity2026} let a large teacher model generate synthetic data and fine-tune small student models for on-device smishing detection, with results that depend strongly on the choice of the teacher. Our Full operating point follows the same routing idea as Chang et al., but with a calibrated character model in front and a compact encoder behind it, and we ask first whether the transformer stage is needed at all.

\subsection{Robustness to Obfuscation}\label{sec:rel-robust}

DeepWordBug \cite{gao2018} and TextBugger \cite{li2019} generate small character and word edits that change the predictions of neural text classifiers. Pruthi et al. \cite{pruthi2019} defend against adversarial misspellings with a word recognition model placed before the classifier, and Boucher et al. \cite{boucher2022} describe imperceptible perturbations with invisible characters, homoglyphs, reorderings and deletions. For short messages, Salman et al. \cite{salman2024} evaluate evasion techniques against SMS spam filters and release the Super SMS Dataset, and Tang and Li \cite{tang2025} find that fine-tuned GPT-2 and BERT spam filters, including one trained on the SMS Spam Collection, are vulnerable to adversarial and data-poisoning attacks. ReCAST \cite{huang2026recast} targets obfuscated Chinese SMS under production latency constraints: it distils the de-obfuscation ability of a large teacher into a smaller student that detects obfuscated spans, predicts their type and restores the text before classification. In this work, the perturbation families used for augmentation and those used only at test time are kept apart, so that robustness is measured against operations that the model has never seen, and the character-level model works on the obfuscated text directly, without a restoration step.

\subsection{Multilingual and Regional SMS Data}\label{sec:rel-data}

Most public SMS corpora are in English. Further corpora include messages reported to Smishtank \cite{timko2024}, the Smishing-4C collection labelled with four smishing categories \cite{smishing4c2024}, MOZ-Smishing with Portuguese mobile-money fraud messages from Mozambique \cite{moz2025}, a Chichewa SMS fraud dataset \cite{taylor2025} and the Azerbaijani SMS Collection with legitimate, promotional and phishing messages \cite{shahbazov2026}. Taylor and Robert \cite{taylor2025} observe a drop in performance when Chichewa messages are replaced by their translations and argue for language-specific models; our case study for Kazakhstan (Section~\ref{sec:kz}) reaches a similar conclusion for Russian and Kazakh.

\subsection{Calibration, Cascades and Early Exit}\label{sec:rel-cascade}

Linear support vector machines over sparse n-gram features are strong text classifiers \cite{cortes1995,joachims1998}, and character n-grams are less sensitive to spelling variation than word features. Sigmoid (Platt) scaling turns their margins into class probabilities \cite{niculescu2005}, which any rule that compares a confidence with a threshold needs; modern neural networks are often poorly calibrated as well \cite{guo2017}. Common baselines for short-text classification are random forests \cite{breiman2001}, gradient boosting \cite{chen2016}, convolutional networks \cite{kim2014} and long short-term memory networks \cite{hochreiter1997}. Cascades of classifiers of increasing cost go back at least to the detector of Viola and Jones \cite{viola2001}, and early-exit networks stop the computation at an intermediate layer when the prediction is confident \cite{teerapittayanon2016,schwartz2020}, with calibrated confidence as the exit criterion in the work of Schwartz et al. \cite{schwartz2020}.

\subsection{Duplicates, Leakage and the Comparison of Classifiers}\label{sec:rel-leak}

Leakage between training and test data, including duplicated records, is a common cause of over-optimistic results in applied machine learning \cite{kapoor2023}. Removing exact and near duplicates changes both the training and the evaluation of language models \cite{lee2022}. Near duplicates are commonly found by comparing sets of character shingles, with MinHash as an efficient approximation of their Jaccard similarity \cite{broder1997}. For the comparison of two classifiers on one test set, Dietterich \cite{dietterich1998} recommends the McNemar test \cite{mcnemar1947}; the bootstrap \cite{efron1993} gives confidence intervals for differences in metrics such as macro F1 that are not simple proportions; and the Holm procedure \cite{holm1979,aickin1996} controls the family-wise error rate when several comparisons are made.
