\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

All numbers in this section are test-set results of the single evaluation after freezing, reported as the mean $\pm$ standard deviation over \val{n-seeds} seeds; significance always refers to the seed-1 predictions with the Holm correction of Section~\ref{sec:stats}. The main tables show the key models; the tables with all models are in Appendix~\ref{app:full}.

\subsection{Published Detectors under Unseen Obfuscation}\label{sec:published}

\input{tables/settings_key}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics{figures/published_robustness}
\caption{Lite, Full and the re-implemented published detectors in their own task settings as a function of the level of held-out obfuscation (operations never used in training). Error bars: standard deviation over \val{n-seeds} seeds.\label{fig:published}}
\end{figure}

The published papers report an accuracy of \val{claim-mh-acc} for the CNN-BiGRU of Mahmud et al. \cite{mahmud2024} and F1 scores between \val{claim-sh-min} and \val{claim-sh-max}\% for the BERT-family models of Shim et al. \cite{shim2024}. These values come from their own random splits without reported deduplication and with an unstated positive class, so they are not directly comparable with ours.

Table~\ref{tab:settings-key} and Figure~\ref{fig:published} show the comparison in the task settings of these papers on our deduplicated data. On clean test data the re-implemented transformers of Shim et al. reach between \val{sh-clean-min} and \val{sh-clean-max} macro F1 and Lite reaches \val{svmchar-shim-clean}; Lite is significantly better than RoBERTa by both tests, but its advantage over BERT and DistilBERT is not significant. Full is significantly better than BERT and DistilBERT by the bootstrap test only and than RoBERTa by both tests. In the Mahmud setting, neither Lite (\val{svmchar-mahmud-clean}) nor Full differs significantly from the CNN-BiGRU (\val{mh-clean}) on clean data.

Under held-out obfuscation the picture changes. At the \val{lvl-c}\% level the three transformers of Shim et al. fall to between \val{sh-ho20-min} and \val{sh-ho20-max} and the CNN-BiGRU to \val{mh-ho20}, while Lite stays at \val{svmchar-shim-ho20} and \val{svmchar-mahmud-ho20} and Full at \val{cascade-shim-ho20} and \val{cascade-mahmud-ho20}. The advantage of Lite is significant by both tests against every published method at the \val{lvl-b}\% and \val{lvl-c}\% levels in the Shim setting and at every held-out level in the Mahmud setting; at the \val{lvl-a}\% level of the Shim setting it is significant only against RoBERTa. Full shows the same pattern, except that at the \val{lvl-a}\% level its difference to DistilBERT is not significant. The standard deviations in Figure~\ref{fig:published} show a second difference: the transformers vary strongly between seeds under obfuscation, while Lite does not. In both settings Full does not differ significantly from the best of our baselines at any level (Table~\ref{tab:significance}). Since Lite alone is as robust as Full, the robustness comes from the character-level model and not from the cascade.

The transformers of Shim et al. are also the slowest detectors in this comparison. On one CPU thread they need a median of \val{lat-sh-p50-min} to \val{lat-sh-p50-max}~ms per message, against \val{lat-svmchar-p50}~ms for Lite, that is, \val{speed-ratio-min} to \val{speed-ratio-max} times more (Table~\ref{tab:operating}).

\subsection{Three-Class Task}\label{sec:main}

\input{tables/key3}

On the clean three-class Mendeley task (Table~\ref{tab:key3}), Lite has the highest macro F1 of all models (\val{svmchar-clean-f1}). Full reaches \val{cascade-clean-f1}~$\pm$~\val{cascade-clean-f1-sd}, practically the same as its first stage alone (\val{svmcharaug-clean-f1}). On seed 1, Full is \val{clean-diff-abs} percentage points below Lite (95\% interval from \val{clean-ci-lo} to \val{clean-ci-hi}); the difference is not significant ($\tilde{p}=\val{clean-best-p-boot-holm}$ for the bootstrap and $\tilde{p}=\val{clean-best-p-mcn-holm}$ for the McNemar test). The fine-tuned transformers are lower on this task: BERT reaches \val{bert-clean-f1} and the second stage of Full alone \val{distilaug-clean-f1}. The few-shot language model is the weakest model (\val{qwen-clean-f1}).

For all models the F1 score of the spam class is lower than that of the smishing class. For Full on seed 1 (Figure~\ref{fig:confusion}), \val{cm-spam-to-smish} of the \val{men-test-spam} spam messages are labelled smishing and \val{cm-smish-to-spam} of the \val{men-test-smishing} smishing messages are labelled spam, while \val{cm-ham-wrong} of the \val{cm-ham-n} ham messages are misclassified; most errors are therefore confusions between the two malicious classes. Full also has a lower calibration error (\val{ece-cascade}) than either of its stages (\val{ece-svmcharaug} and \val{ece-distilaug}). On the clean Super SMS corpus all trained models lie between \val{ss-min} and \val{ss-max}; Full (\val{ss-cascade}) and the best baseline, BERT (\val{ss-bert}), do not differ significantly ($\tilde{p}=\val{ss-p-boot}$ and $\val{ss-p-mcn}$).

\subsection{Robustness in the Three-Class Task}\label{sec:robustness}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics{figures/obfuscation_robustness}
\caption{Macro F1 on the three-class test set as a function of the obfuscation level, for the family used in augmentation (TR, left) and the held-out family (HO, right). Key models; all models in Table~\ref{tab:robustness}.\label{fig:obfuscation}}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:obfuscation} and Table~\ref{tab:robustness} show the three-class results under obfuscation. The realised share of edited characters matches the nominal level (for example \val{realised-ho20}\% at the \val{lvl-c}\% held-out level). Models that work on word or subword tokens without augmentation degrade strongly: at the \val{lvl-c}\% held-out level, BERT, the convolutional and recurrent networks, the tuned DistilBERT and the first gated fusion design reach only between \val{rob-neural-ho20-min} and \val{rob-neural-ho20-max}. Augmentation with the TR family carries over to the unseen HO family for the transformer: with the same architecture, the second stage of Full reaches \val{rob-distilaug-ho20} with augmentation and \val{rob-distil-ho20} without it. The character-level models change little under either family: Lite, which was trained without any augmentation, stays at \val{rob-svmchar-ho20}, and Full at \val{rob-cascade-ho20}. The share of messages that Full routes to the transformer grows only slightly, from \val{routed-clean-pct}\% on the clean test set to at most \val{routed-max-pct}\%. Against the best baseline of each obfuscated test set, Full is never significantly better (Table~\ref{tab:significance}); at the \val{lvl-a}\% TR level it is significantly worse by the bootstrap test ($\tilde{p}=\val{tr05-p-boot}$), but not by the McNemar test.

\subsection{Speed of the Operating Points}\label{sec:speed}

\input{tables/operating}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics{figures/quality_vs_latency}
\caption{Macro F1 on the clean three-class test set against the median latency per message on one CPU thread; the dashed line is the Pareto front.\label{fig:pareto}}
\end{figure}

Measured end to end on all \val{men-test-n} test messages, Full has a median latency of \val{lat-cascade-p50}~ms, a 95th percentile of \val{lat-cascade-p95}~ms and a throughput of \val{lat-cascade-thr} messages per second on one CPU thread, with \val{lat-cascade-routed-pct}\% of the messages sent to the second stage (Table~\ref{tab:operating}). Its mean latency, \val{lat-cascade-mean}~ms, is about twice the median, because each routed message costs a full transformer call. Lite has a median of \val{lat-svmchar-p50}~ms and a throughput of \val{lat-svmchar-thr} messages per second; DistilBERT needs \val{lat-distilaug-p50}~ms and BERT \val{lat-bert-p50}~ms. The few-shot language model needs \val{lat-qwen-p50}~ms per message even on a GPU.

Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} places these results on the quality-latency plane. Lite is on the Pareto front. Full lies slightly below it in clean macro F1 and needs more time and memory, mainly the \val{size-cascade}~MB of the transformer weights, against \val{size-svmchar}~MB for Lite. On clean data, Full thus brings no measurable gain in quality over Lite; what it adds is a transformer fallback for the messages on which the character model is not confident, at a mean cost per message that stays within a few milliseconds, and the lowest error cost under strong obfuscation (Section~\ref{sec:routing}).

\subsection{Routing, Error Cost and Ablations of Full}\label{sec:routing}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics{figures/cascade_share_vs_f1}
\caption{Macro F1 of Full when a given share of the test messages with the lowest first-stage confidence is sent to the second stage (descriptive, not used for any choice). HO: held-out obfuscation family; dotted line: share sent at the frozen threshold.\label{fig:share}}
\end{figure}

\emph{What the second stage contributes.} Figure~\ref{fig:share} varies the share of test messages sent to the second stage, always choosing those with the lowest first-stage confidence. On the clean test set the macro F1 rises from \val{curve-clean-0} with no routing to \val{curve-clean-peak} at \val{curve-clean-peak-share}\% and then falls to \val{curve-clean-100} when every message goes to the transformer; the held-out sets behave similarly. A small share of uncertain messages thus benefits from the transformer, and the frozen threshold, with \val{lat-cascade-routed-pct}\% on the test set, lies in this region. The curve is computed on the test set and is descriptive only.

\emph{Cost of errors.} Table~\ref{tab:efficiency} gives the loss $L_r$ of (\ref{eq:cost}) for all models. On the clean test set the lowest loss for every $r$ belongs to the average of the two stages, an ablation (\val{cost-clean-r3-average} for $r=\val{cost-r3}$). Among the other models Full has the lowest loss for $r=\val{cost-r1}$ (\val{cost-clean-r1-cascade}), and the second stage alone for $r=\val{cost-r2}$ and $r=\val{cost-r3}$, with Full close to it (\val{cost-clean-r3-cascade} against \val{cost-clean-r3-distilaug} for $r=\val{cost-r3}$). At the \val{lvl-c}\% held-out level Full has the lowest loss of all models for every $r$; for $r=\val{cost-r2}$ it is \val{cost-ho20-r2-cascade} against \val{cost-ho20-r2-svmcharaug} for its first stage and \val{cost-ho20-r2-distilaug} for its second stage. Full is not the cheapest everywhere: in the Mahmud setting at the same level its loss for $r=\val{cost-r2}$ is \val{cost-mh-ho20-r2-cascade} against \val{cost-mh-ho20-r2-svmcharaug} for the augmented character model, because there it mostly answers with the transformer. Differences in $L_r$ were not tested for significance.

\emph{Ablations.} The cascade of the two models trained without augmentation (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}) has a higher mean macro F1 than Full on \val{noaug-wins} of the \val{n-rob-sets} three-class test sets, all except the \val{lvl-b}\% held-out set; only \val{noaug-beyond} of these differences exceeds the seed spread. Augmentation therefore did not improve the cascade on test, although it is essential for the transformer alone. Averaging the probabilities of the two stages is lower than Full under every obfuscation level. Two earlier designs of ours, which fuse a DistilBERT representation with hand-crafted features through a learned gate, are not better than Lite on any test set; the second, with character features and augmentation, is robust (\val{rob-v2-ho20} at the \val{lvl-c}\% held-out level) but about \val{v2-slowdown} times slower than Full.

\subsection{Low Data and Transfer}\label{sec:lowdata}

With less training data (Table~\ref{tab:lowdata} and Figure~\ref{fig:learning} in Appendix~\ref{app:full}), Lite has the highest mean macro F1 among the baselines at every fraction, and Full falls behind it. (The average of the two stages, an ablation, is higher at \val{ld-a}\% and \val{ld-b}\%, with \val{ld01-average} and \val{ld05-average}.) With \val{ld-c}\% of the data Full reaches \val{ld10-cascade} against \val{ld10-svmchar} for Lite, a significant difference by both tests in favour of Lite; at \val{ld-b}\% the difference is significant by the bootstrap test only. At \val{ld-a}\% the McNemar test on seed 1 favours Full (\val{ld01-b} messages correct only for Full against \val{ld01-c}), but the bootstrap test is not significant and the mean over seeds is lower (\val{ld01-cascade} against \val{ld01-svmchar}). The frozen threshold sends \val{ld01-share}\% of the messages to the second stage at \val{ld-a}\% of the data, where the transformer is weak (\val{ld01-distilaug}).

Transfer from English to Portuguese without target data fails for all models: the best mean macro F1 is \val{tr-zs-best}. With the threshold chosen on the source validation data ($\tau=\val{tau-tr-zs}$), Full sends \val{tr-zs-share}\% of the messages to the second stage and is identical to it on seed 1. After fine-tuning on \val{tr-frac}\% of the target training data, Full reaches \val{tr-ft-cascade} against \val{tr-ft-distilaug} for its second stage alone, a difference that is significant by the bootstrap test only ($\tilde{p}=\val{tr-ft-p-boot}$); the average of the two stages has the highest mean (\val{tr-ft-average}) but was not tested. Here most messages reach the transformer (\val{tr-ft-share}\%), so Full saves no computation. Across all conditions and test sets, Full was significantly better than the best baseline in two comparisons, the transfer after fine-tuning and the \val{ld-a}\% low-data condition, each time by one of the two tests (Table~\ref{tab:significance}).

\subsection{Duplicates and Label Noise}\label{sec:duplicates}

\input{tables/inflation}

Table~\ref{tab:inflation} compares five-fold cross-validation on the raw Mendeley data with the same procedure on our deduplicated data. In the binary ham-versus-malicious task, the raw data give higher scores: for the CNN-BiGRU, accuracy is higher by \val{infl-mh-acc} and macro F1 by \val{infl-mh-f1} percentage points, and the raw-data accuracy (\val{infl-mh-acc-dirty}) is close to the value reported in the original paper (\val{claim-mh-acc}). In the three-class and smishing-versus-spam tasks the raw data give \emph{lower} scores, by up to \val{infl-max-drop} points of macro F1. The raw data keep both members of the conflicting smishing and spam pairs, which give contradictory training and test labels; in the binary task both labels are malicious and the conflict disappears. The analysis therefore mixes two effects, duplicates and conflicting labels, which we did not separate. It shows that the treatment of duplicates changes the scores by several points in either direction, more than most differences between models in Table~\ref{tab:key3}.

The boundary between the two malicious classes is not labelled consistently in the Mendeley corpus. All \val{men-conflict-groups} groups of duplicates with conflicting labels pair a smishing and a spam label on the same or nearly the same text, and of the \val{men-uci-smish} deduplicated Mendeley smishing messages, \val{men-smish-uci-spam} have a duplicate in the UCI collection, where they are labelled spam. Part of the confusion between these classes is therefore likely to come from the labels rather than from the models.

\subsection{Case Study: Kazakhstan}\label{sec:kz}

The models above were trained on English messages. To see how they behave in a different context, we wrote a small set of messages for Kazakhstan, where text messages are commonly written in Russian or Kazakh. This part is a qualitative demonstration with a few dozen messages, not a statistical evaluation, and the translation and adaptation experiments were added after the main results (post hoc).

\emph{Messages.} Seven scenarios (a bank card blocked, a prize, an expiring digital signature, a parcel held for payment, early pension withdrawal, a bonus for a code, a call from a bank security service) each have a fraudulent message and a legitimate counterpart, written in English, Russian and Kazakh (\val{kz-orig} messages). For safety, all links point to the reserved domain \texttt{.example} and all phone numbers are fictitious. Fraudulent Russian messages also appear with the held-out obfuscation and fraudulent Russian and Kazakh messages with Cyrillic letters replaced by Latin look-alikes (\val{kz-all} messages in total). We tested Lite, Full and BERT on the messages as written and on English translations by NLLB-200 \cite{nllb2024}.

\emph{As written and translated.} Russian and Kazakh are outside the training distribution: Lite and Full label \val{kz-ru-missed-lite} of the \val{kz-fraud-lang} Russian and \val{kz-kk-missed-lite} of the \val{kz-fraud-lang} Kazakh fraudulent messages as ham, and BERT \val{kz-ru-missed-bert} and \val{kz-kk-missed-bert}. Even in English, Lite labels only \val{kz-en-correct-lite} of the \val{kz-en-n} messages correctly, which points to a shift in domain and not only in language. Translating the whole message helps for Kazakh, where no fraudulent message is labelled ham afterwards, but less for Russian, where \val{kz-ru-tr-missed-min} to \val{kz-ru-tr-missed-max} remain missed. The translation model often translates only the first sentence: the link disappears in \val{kz-link-lost-word} of the \val{kz-link-n} Russian and Kazakh messages that contain one. Translating sentence by sentence with links, numbers and codes protected by placeholders keeps every link and raises the number of correct labels of Lite for the original Russian messages from \val{kz-ru-sent-before} to \val{kz-ru-sent-after} of \val{kz-ru-orig-n}, but not for Kazakh (from \val{kz-kk-sent-before} to \val{kz-kk-sent-after}). Translation costs a median of \val{kz-mt-p50}~s per message for whole messages and \val{kz-mts-p50}~s sentence by sentence on one CPU thread, three orders of magnitude more than classification.

\emph{Adaptation.} We retrained Lite on the Mendeley training data together with a synthetic adaptation set of \val{kz-adapt-n} messages (\val{kz-adapt-ideas} message ideas in three languages, drafted with a language model and checked by the authors, with no near duplicates of the case-study messages). With the whole set (condition B), the fraudulent case-study messages labelled smishing rise from \val{kz-fraud-before} to \val{kz-fraud-after-b} of \val{kz-fraud-n}, and the share of correct labels from \val{kz-acc-before} to \val{kz-acc-after-b} (Figure~\ref{fig:adaptation} in Appendix~\ref{app:full}). Using only message categories that do not occur in the case study (condition A) gives \val{kz-fraud-after-a} of \val{kz-fraud-n} and \val{kz-acc-after-a}. The macro F1 on the Mendeley validation data stays between \val{kz-val-min} and \val{kz-val-max}, against \val{kz-val-before} before adaptation, and the latency does not change. The price is false alarms. On the legitimate adaptation messages with links that were not used for training, the adapted models label between \val{kz-legit-link-min} and \val{kz-legit-link-max} correctly, about as many as before adaptation (\val{kz-legit-link-before}), but the errors move from spam to smishing: \val{kz-legit-link-smish-after}\% of these messages are flagged as smishing after adaptation against \val{kz-legit-link-smish-before}\% before. The genuine bank warning about a suspicious transaction is flagged as smishing by the adapted model in all three languages, and by all three models after translation. Because the adaptation set and the case-study messages were written in a similar style, these effects may be overestimated.

\emph{Real messages.} In public sources (news articles) we found only \val{kz-real-n} verbatim texts of fraudulent messages sent in Kazakhstan, one of them a fragment and one cut off in a screenshot. As written, the adapted Lite labels \val{kz-real-flagged} of them smishing, with a confidence between \val{kz-real-conf-min} and \val{kz-real-conf-max}; the models without adaptation, Full and BERT label none of them smishing. After sentence-wise translation some of them are recognised by the other models, but \val{kz-real-never} message, a fragment that only reports a change in the status of a loan and contains no link or request, is labelled smishing by no model with either input. With so few messages this is an illustration only.
