\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why the Character-Level Model Is Robust}\label{sec:why}

Three observations explain the robustness of Lite. First, its evidence is spread over many overlapping n-grams (Section~\ref{sec:explain}). One character belongs to at most \val{ngrams-per-char} of the character n-grams of lengths \val{char-ng-lo} to \val{char-ng-hi}, so an edit at the level $\lambda$ changes only the n-grams around the edited positions, and the n-grams of the unedited parts of the message keep their weights. An n-gram that the edit creates is usually absent from the vocabulary or has a small weight, so it removes evidence rather than adding misleading evidence. Second, subword and word models see the same edit differently: a doubled letter or a Cyrillic look-alike can split a word into unfamiliar pieces, and the whole representation of the word changes \cite{pruthi2019,boucher2022}. This is consistent with the strong degradation of BERT, DistilBERT and the published transformers without augmentation (Sections~\ref{sec:published} and~\ref{sec:robustness}). Third, the robustness of Lite does not come from augmentation: it was trained on clean messages only. Augmentation helps the transformer (\val{rob-distilaug-ho20} against \val{rob-distil-ho20} at the \val{lvl-c}\% held-out level), but it does not close the gap to the character-level model, and it did not improve the cascade. These are explanations consistent with our results, not tested causes.

\subsection{When to Use Lite and When Full}\label{sec:when}

Lite is the natural choice for a handset. On the clean three-class test set it has the highest macro F1 of all models, its quality changes little under every obfuscation we applied, it needs \val{lat-svmchar-p50}~ms per message on one CPU thread and \val{size-svmchar}~MB of storage, and it needs neither a GPU nor a network connection, so the message never leaves the device.

Full is meant for an operator gateway, where a transformer can be afforded for a small share of the traffic. In the three-class task it sends \val{lat-cascade-routed-pct}\% of the messages to DistilBERT, keeps the median latency at \val{lat-cascade-p50}~ms and has the lowest error cost under the strongest held-out obfuscation, although this difference in cost was not tested for significance. On clean data it does not differ significantly from Lite. Its value depends on the confidence of the first stage: when the first stage is often unsure, as with little training data or after a change of language, the frozen threshold sends most messages to the transformer (up to \val{tr-ft-share}\% in the transfer experiment), and Full inherits the cost and the weaknesses of the transformer. A gateway should therefore deploy Full only where its first stage is confident on most of the traffic, and use Lite elsewhere.

\subsection{Recommendations for Banks and Mobile Operators}\label{sec:recommend}

The results suggest the following practice.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Evaluate on deduplicated data and on unseen obfuscation.} Duplicates changed cross-validated macro F1 by up to \val{infl-max-drop} points, more than most differences between models, and published detectors that look strong on clean data lost much of their quality on obfuscation they had not seen. A detector should be tested on obfuscation operations that were not used in its training, and its latency should be reported with its accuracy.
\item \emph{Use a calibrated character-level model as the first line.} It is fast, small and robust, it can run on the handset or at the gateway, and its calibrated probabilities make thresholds and cost ratios meaningful.
\item \emph{Call a transformer only for uncertain messages, and watch the routed share.} The share of messages routed to the second stage grew from about \val{lat-cascade-routed-pct}\% in the three-class task to between \val{routed-binary-min-pct}\% and \val{routed-binary-max-pct}\% in settings where the first stage was less certain. A rising share can therefore serve as a cheap signal that the incoming traffic has drifted away from the training data.
\item \emph{Retrain with local data.} Many of the strongest cues of Lite, such as the digit patterns of phone numbers and the short numbers of subscription services, belong to the senders and numbering plans of the training corpus. A bank or an operator should retrain on recent labelled messages from its own traffic, which is cheap for a linear model.
\item \emph{Expect false alarms on genuine warnings.} After adaptation, Lite flagged a genuine bank warning about a suspicious transaction as smishing in every language. Legitimate notifications and fraudulent ones share their vocabulary, so senders of genuine notifications benefit from being identifiable by other means, such as registered sender names.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Implications for Regions such as Kazakhstan}\label{sec:region}

In countries where text messages are written in Russian, Kazakh and other languages, models trained on English corpora miss most fraudulent messages (Section~\ref{sec:kz}). Machine translation recovers part of them, but it takes seconds per message, loses links when it translates only the first sentence, and helps the two languages unequally. A small synthetic adaptation set raises recall but moves errors from spam to smishing, so it shifts the problem towards false alarms. A character-level model has an advantage here: it needs no tokenizer for a particular language or script and can be retrained cheaply as soon as local data exist. What is missing are such data. Public SMS corpora in regional languages are beginning to appear, for example in Azerbaijani \cite{shahbazov2026} and Chichewa \cite{taylor2025}, and our results support the conclusion of Taylor and Robert \cite{taylor2025} that language-specific data are preferable to translation. Our obfuscation model also needs extension for these languages, because on Cyrillic text the held-out family reduces mostly to letter doubling.

\subsection{Why Not a Large Language Model}\label{sec:llm}

Large language models are a natural candidate for detecting scams \cite{salman2025spallm,topcuoglu2026}, but three reasons speak against them as the main detector in this setting. \emph{Latency and cost}: the few-shot Qwen2.5-7B model needed \val{lat-qwen-p50}~ms per message even on a GPU, against \val{lat-svmchar-p50}~ms for Lite on one CPU thread, and an operator gateway has to classify every message. \emph{Quality without task-specific training}: it was the weakest model on the clean three-class task (\val{qwen-clean-f1}), in line with the finding that zero-shot and few-shot prompting is less reliable than fine-tuning for SMS spam \cite{salman2025spallm}. \emph{Privacy}: a hosted model receives the content of private messages, and a local model of this size needs \val{size-qwen-gb}~GB of weights even in 4-bit precision. Fine-tuned language models generalise better to unseen scams than fine-tuned encoders in some studies \cite{topcuoglu2026}, and hierarchical systems call them only for ambiguous messages \cite{chang2025scamshield}. This is the same design as Full, with a language model in place of DistilBERT; our results suggest that such a second stage should be justified by its gain on hard and shifted messages, not by its accuracy on clean benchmarks, and we evaluated only one few-shot model and no fine-tuned language model.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

The obfuscation in this study is random and non-adaptive, so robustness against an attacker who targets the detector, paraphrases the message or uses invisible characters remains to be tested; adaptive attacks are the main direction of future work. Languages other than English and Portuguese were studied only in a small qualitative case study, and an evaluation on real labelled messages in Russian, Kazakh and other languages is future work as well.
